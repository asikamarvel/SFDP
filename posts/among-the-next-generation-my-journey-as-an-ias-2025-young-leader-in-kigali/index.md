*How** **a** **week** **in** **Rwanda's** **'Land** **of** **a** **Thousand** **Hills'** **reshaped** **my** **vision** **for** the future of HIV response*

**By Marvellous Oluebube Asika, MLS **Communication and Engagement Associate Society For Disease Prevention Inc., USA

July 2025

# The Email That Changed Everything

The notification sound on my phone was unremarkable—just another email in an already crowded inbox. But as I read the subject line, "Congratulations! IAS 2025 Young Leaders Programme," my heart stopped for a moment. After months of anticipation, there it was: my acceptance into one of the most prestigious leadership programs in global HIV response.

Being selected as one of only 25-30 Young Leaders from around the world felt surreal. At 25, I had dedicated my career to public health through my work at the Society For Disease Prevention Inc., but this recognition as a global changemaker under 25 carried a weight I hadn't fully prepared for. The email detailed the incredible opportunity ahead: meetings with leading scientists, innovation workshops, career forums, and networking with fellow young activists and researchers from across the globe.

As I sat in my office in the United States, looking out at the familiar skyline, I couldn't help but think about the journey that had brought me here. From my

early days studying medical laboratory science to my current role in communication and engagement, every step had been building toward something bigger. This invitation to Kigali wasn't just a professional opportunity—it was a calling to join the next generation of leaders who would shape the future of HIV prevention and treatment worldwide.

# Arriving in the Land of a Thousand Hills

The descent into Kigali Gasogi International Airport offered my first glimpse of why Rwanda is called the "Land of a Thousand Hills." As our plane banked through the early morning mist, endless green ridges rolled out like a verdant carpet, punctuated by the gleaming towers of Kigali's modern downtown. There was something profoundly symbolic about hosting IAS 2025 here, in the heart of Africa—a continent that has borne the brunt of the HIV pandemic but has also shown remarkable resilience and leadership in response.

The Kigali Convention Centre stood as a testament to Rwanda's transformation and progress. Its sleek, modern architecture seemed to embody the forward-looking spirit of both the country and the conference we were about to attend. As I walked through the entrance with my luggage and a head full of expectations, I was struck by the international tapestry of delegates streaming in—scientists from South Africa, activists from Thailand, researchers from Brazil, community workers from Uganda. This wasn't just a conference; it was a global gathering of minds united by a common cause.

My first Young Leaders meetup was scheduled for that evening, and as I entered the designated room, I was immediately overwhelmed by the diversity and passion of my fellow selectees. There was Amara, a 24-year-old researcher from Nigeria working on adolescent HIV prevention; James, a 23-year-old activist from Jamaica advocating for LGBTQ+ rights and HIV awareness; and Priya, a 26-year-old epidemiologist from India developing innovative testing strategies

for rural communities. Each introduction revealed another layer of the complex, global nature of HIV response, and I realized I was in the presence of the future leaders of this movement.

# A Curriculum Like No Other

What struck me most about the Young Leaders Programme was its intentional design. This wasn't a traditional conference experience where young professionals sat in the back of large auditoriums taking notes. Instead, the curriculum was tailored specifically to strengthen our networking, research, and advocacy skills while positioning us as active contributors to the global HIV response.

The programme balanced structured learning with organic relationship-building in a way I had never experienced before. Each session was designed not just to inform us about the latest developments in HIV science, but to help us understand how we could contribute to and eventually lead these developments. The facilitators treated us not as students, but as emerging leaders whose perspectives and innovations were valuable to the broader HIV community.

What made this experience unique was the recognition that young people bring fresh perspectives, technological fluency, and cultural insights that are essential for reaching populations most at risk. The curriculum acknowledged that we weren't just the future of HIV response—we were the present, with vital contributions to make immediately.

# Meeting Giants: Sessions with Leading Scientists and Advocates

One of the most transformative aspects of the Young Leaders Programme was the unprecedented access we had to the luminaries of HIV research and advocacy. These weren't brief handshakes or formal presentations, but intimate conversations with the people whose work has shaped decades of HIV response.

I'll never forget my conversation with Dr. Sarah Fidler, whose research on HIV cure strategies has been groundbreaking. What struck me wasn't just her scientific brilliance, but her genuine curiosity about our perspectives as young professionals. She asked probing questions about how we thought social media and digital technologies could enhance treatment adherence among young people—questions that showed she understood we brought valuable insights to the table.

Similarly, my session with Winnie Byanyima, Executive Director of UNAIDS, challenged many of my assumptions about global health leadership. Her discussion of the intersection between HIV response and broader social justice issues expanded my understanding of how public health work must address structural inequalities. She emphasized that technical expertise alone wasn't enough—we needed to understand the political and social contexts that either facilitate or hinder health interventions.

These interactions weren't just inspiring; they were paradigm-shifting. These leaders demonstrated that effective HIV response requires both scientific rigor and deep humanity, both technical innovation and community engagement. They modeled a kind of leadership that I aspired to emulate in my own career trajectory.

# Innovation Workshops: Where Ideas Take Flight

The innovation workshops were where the magic really happened. Bringing together 25 young minds from different continents, disciplines, and lived experiences created an environment ripe for breakthrough thinking. The facilitators structured these sessions around real-world challenges in HIV prevention and treatment, asking us to develop creative solutions collaboratively.

During one particularly memorable workshop, we were tasked with addressing the challenge of HIV testing uptake among adolescents in resource-limited settings. Our diverse team included a social media strategist from Kenya, a behavioral scientist from Canada, a community health worker from Malawi, and myself, bringing communications expertise from the United States. What emerged from our brainstorming was a comprehensive approach that combined peer-to-peer education, mobile testing units, and gamified health apps—solutions that none of us could have developed in isolation.

The energy in these workshops was electric. Ideas built upon ideas, cultural insights challenged assumptions, and innovative approaches emerged from the intersection of different perspectives. I watched teams form organically around shared interests and complementary skills, partnerships that I knew would extend far beyond the conference walls.

What impressed me most was how these sessions balanced ambition with practicality. We weren't just brainstorming pie-in-the-sky solutions; we were developing implementable strategies grounded in real-world constraints and opportunities. The facilitators pushed us to consider not just what was possible, but what was feasible, sustainable, and culturally appropriate.

# The Career Forum: Charting Individual Pathways

The one-on-one career forum was perhaps the most personally transformative element of the entire programme. Being paired with Dr. Mitchell Warren, a veteran of HIV advocacy and prevention research, provided me with insights that I couldn't have gained any other way. Our conversation wasn't just about my current role at SFDP, but about the arc of a career in public health and how to maximize impact over time.

Dr. Warren helped me understand how my background in medical laboratory science, combined with my current communications work, positioned me uniquely to bridge the gap between scientific research and public understanding. He encouraged me to think beyond traditional career pathways and consider how I might create hybrid roles that leverage multiple skill sets. His advice about the importance of international experience, cross-sector collaboration, and continuous learning shaped my thinking about future opportunities.

What struck me most about this mentorship was the generosity of time and wisdom. Dr. Warren didn't just offer generic advice; he provided specific, actionable guidance tailored to my interests and goals. He helped me see how my work at SFDP could serve as a launching pad for broader contributions to global health, and he connected my daily responsibilities to larger questions about health equity and social justice.

The career forum also provided practical guidance about navigating the complexities of public health careers. From funding challenges to interdisciplinary collaboration, from academic partnerships to policy advocacy,

Dr. Warren helped me understand the landscape I was entering and the skills I would need to develop to be effective in it.

# Networking That Transcends Borders

The connections I made during the Young Leaders Programme extended far beyond formal sessions and structured activities. Some of the most meaningful conversations happened during coffee breaks, over shared meals, and in the quiet moments between official events. These organic interactions created bonds that I knew would strengthen the global HIV response for years to come.

I was particularly inspired by my conversations with Maria, a young researcher from Brazil working on HIV prevention among indigenous communities. Her insights about culturally appropriate health interventions and community-based participatory research challenged me to think more critically about how Western approaches to public health might need to be adapted for different cultural contexts. Our late-night discussions about decolonizing global health practices opened my eyes to perspectives I hadn't fully considered before.

Similarly, my friendship with Taiwo, a community activist from Ghana working with sex workers, provided me with ground-level insights into the realities of HIV prevention work. His stories about the daily challenges faced by marginalized communities made the statistics and research findings I work with at SFDP feel more human and urgent. His passion for ensuring that research serves the needs of the communities most affected by HIV inspired me to think more critically about how my communication work can center the voices of those we aim to serve.

By the end of the programme, we had established multiple WhatsApp groups, shared email lists, and collaborative document folders. But more than that, we had created a support network of peers who understood the unique challenges

and opportunities of being young professionals in global HIV response. This network has already proven invaluable as we navigate our careers and seek to implement the innovations and partnerships we developed together.

# Beyond the Conference Halls

Some of the most impactful learning happened outside the formal conference programming. Exploring Kigali with fellow Young Leaders provided opportunities for cultural exchange and informal mentorship that enriched the entire experience. Rwanda's remarkable progress in HIV response and overall development served as a living case study in effective leadership and community mobilization.

During a visit to the Kigali Genocide Memorial with several other Young Leaders, we had profound conversations about resilience, healing, and the importance of learning from history to prevent future tragedies. These discussions deepened our understanding of how trauma-informed approaches might enhance HIV prevention and care, particularly for communities that have experienced violence and displacement.

Our informal mentorship sessions over Rwandan coffee were equally valuable. Senior researchers and advocates who were attending the conference made themselves available for impromptu conversations that provided insights into career development, research methodologies, and advocacy strategies. These casual interactions often proved more illuminating than formal presentations, offering unvarnished perspectives on the challenges and rewards of careers in global health.

The setting in Africa was particularly meaningful for centering African leadership and innovation in global HIV response. Too often, conferences about African health issues happen in Europe or North America, inadvertently reinforcing colonial dynamics. Hosting IAS 2025 in Kigali sent a powerful message about whose knowledge and leadership are valued in addressing HIV globally.

# Carrying Forward the SFDP Mission

Throughout the Young Leaders Programme, I was conscious of representing not just myself, but the Society For Disease Prevention Inc. and the values we champion. The experience reinforced my appreciation for SFDP's commitment

to evidence-based prevention strategies and community engagement, while also providing new perspectives on how we might enhance our work.

The programme's emphasis on youth engagement aligned perfectly with SFDP's recognition that young people are not just beneficiaries of public health interventions, but essential partners in developing and implementing them. The innovations and partnerships I witnessed in Kigali demonstrated the power of intergenerational collaboration and the unique contributions that young professionals bring to global health challenges.

I returned to my role at SFDP with concrete ideas for enhancing our disease prevention strategies. The community-based approaches I learned about from fellow Young Leaders have informed my thinking about how we might improve our engagement with marginalized communities. The digital health innovations discussed in our workshops have inspired new approaches to health communication and behavior change.

Perhaps most importantly, the programme reinforced the importance of SFDP's mission to build capacity among young health professionals. My experience as a Young Leader demonstrated the transformative power of investing in emerging talent, providing mentorship and networking opportunities, and creating platforms for youth leadership in global health.

# The Lasting Impact

As I reflect on the week in Kigali, I'm struck by how profoundly the experience has shaped my understanding of my role in global HIV response. Being recognized as a Young Leader came with responsibilities that extend far beyond my individual career aspirations. I now see myself as part of a global network of emerging leaders who have the opportunity—and obligation—to transform how the world responds to HIV.

The relationships I formed with fellow Young Leaders have already begun to influence my work at SFDP. We're collaborating on research projects, sharing resources  and  best  practices,  and  supporting  each  other's  professional

development in ways that strengthen all of our individual contributions. This network effect multiplies the impact of the investment that IAS made in each of us.

The mentorship I received has provided me with a clearer vision of how to navigate the intersection of science, advocacy, and policy in my career. I'm more confident about pursuing leadership opportunities, more strategic about building the skills and relationships necessary for impact, and more committed to paying forward the support I received by mentoring other young professionals.

Perhaps most importantly, the programme reinforced my commitment to ensuring that HIV response remains grounded in the experiences and needs of the communities most affected. The voices of people living with HIV, sex workers, LGBTQ+ individuals, people who use drugs, and other marginalized communities must remain central to research priorities, policy decisions, and programmatic innovations.

# A Beginning, Not an End

The final session of the Young Leaders Programme was bittersweet. As we gathered for our closing ceremony, there was palpable emotion in the room—gratitude for the opportunities we had received, excitement about the collaborations we had formed, and determination to live up to the expectations that came with being selected as leaders in this movement.

The week in Kigali wasn't just a professional development opportunity; it was a transformative experience that reshaped my understanding of my role in global health. I left Rwanda with new friends, mentors, and collaborators from around the world, but more than that, I left with a renewed sense of purpose and responsibility.

The investment that the International AIDS Society made in our cohort of Young Leaders represents a bet on the future of HIV response. It's a recognition that ending the HIV pandemic will require fresh perspectives, innovative approaches, and diverse leadership. It's also an acknowledgment that the challenges we face—from funding cuts to political backlash to persistent stigma

—require sustained, strategic action by leaders who are committed for the long haul.

As I continue my work at SFDP and pursue additional opportunities in global health, I'm committed to paying forward the mentorship I received in Kigali. The senior researchers and advocates who invested their time and wisdom in our development demonstrated the importance of intergenerational collaboration in sustaining social movements. I'm determined to provide similar support to other young professionals entering this field.

The HIV pandemic has already lasted more than four decades, affecting millions of lives and testing the resilience of communities worldwide. But it has also demonstrated the power of scientific innovation, community mobilization, and sustained advocacy to transform what was once a universally fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition for those with access to treatment.

The Young Leaders Programme reminded me that we stand on the shoulders of giants—activists, researchers, clinicians, and community organizers who refused to accept that HIV was unstoppable. Our generation of leaders inherits both their legacy and their unfinished work. The connections made in Kigali, the innovations developed in our workshops, and the commitments we made to each other and our communities represent the next chapter in this ongoing story of resilience and hope.
